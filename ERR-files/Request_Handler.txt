Draft Archetypes: How and Why

Starting around Theros block and increasingly up to the present day, the ten draft archetypes has become a big part of how canon sets are designed. As a visible part of set structure, I find designers are often drawn to them, as they should be. Draft archetypes are a useful tool but are easy to misunderstand.

To be clear, I am talking about the ten deck ideas, each for a different two-color pair, that are clearly identified by the gold uncommons and show up on inserts in prerelease kits and on articles and videos made by WotC. Constructed players and designers use the word "archetype" (or "macro-archetype") to describe the few broad categories of constructed deck (approximately, aggro, tempo, midrange, ramp, control, and combo).

A typical draft archetype has a pretty small footprint. Taking FRA's WU surveil as an example, there is 1 common, 4 uncommons, and 3 rares that trigger whenever you surveil (or scry, but there's no scry in this set). 1 or 2 common payoffs is typical, although common usually has the highest density of enablers, in this case cards that surveil. Uncommon is where the archetypes are most prevalent, usually at least one gold uncommon and 1 uncommon in each color. The number of rares and mythics can vary a lot; FRA's GW lifegain has 5 rares and mythics while DSK's UR rooms had 0.

<b>Why use Draft Archetypes</b>

Daft archetypes have become enough pat of Magic's identity that they can easy to take for granted, but to use them well it's impotant to undestand why they exist.

The most important job of a draft archetype is to guide new players (new to draft or just new to your set) through the draft. Since your custom set is going to be drafted a lot less than a canon set, making those first few drafts good is even more important. Suppose Timmy sits down for his first draft of your set. Timmy isn't very good at card evaluation, but he sees a red-white gold uncommon with the phrase "attack with exactly two creatures" on it. He decides to pick it, and from this point forward picks every card he can find with those words on it, picking whatever red or white card seems most appealing when no such option is present. At the end of the draft, Timmy has a functional deck with a real game plan and some synergy. He goes 1-2, but one game he manages to attack with two creatures to trigger three different cards and hit his opponent for 12 in one turn, and that feels pretty good. 

Did Timmy draft optimally? Of course not, the optimal drafting strategy was to stay open with a common black removal spell pick one, realize green was open pick 4, and end up in a black-green deck without much synergy but benefiting from being passed some strong uncommons pack 3 due to being in the open lane. But Timmy had a good time regardless.

Draft archetypes add value to the set even for stronger drafters, most notably by making decks feel distinct from each other. Having these synergy packages can make the experience of drafting and playing WU blink feel different than that of drafting UB threshold (or even a WU deck that didn't get a strong blink theme). This gives a set more variety and replayability on repeat drafts. Draft archetypes also improve signaling by making cards have different values to different decks, allowing skilled drafters to identify and be rewarded for identifying not only open colors but open pairs.

Draft archetypes can also impact how your set feels for constructed and at rare and mythic. Every set you have to make new cards. Some of those are going to be cards that could have been in any set but you came up with them for this one. Many of them are using new mechanics, which makes them automatically something novel. But a good chunk of them are cards that contain text for an archetype. Before EOE there weren't many cards that said "if you control two or more tapped creatures", but it was the RW theme so they got to make some and have it feel natural. Sometimes these archetypes can even be pushed as a constructed thing.

<b>Criteria of a Good Draft Archetype</b>

It is important that it be really obvious what cards are in your archetype. The clearest version of this is for the archetype to have some word or phrase Timmy can look for - "Knight", "noncreature spell", "where X is the number of creature cards in your graveyard". It can also work on a slightly more subtle level - cards that care about sacrificing artifacts go with cheap artifacts with enter triggers, cards that want you to double-spell go with cheap cards that replace themselves. You can have some less obvious archetypes as long as there's a few really overt ones for Timmy to latch on to. 

What you want to avoid is "RW aggro" or "UB control". Timmy, who has only ever played planechase judge's tower commander, doesn't know what an aggro deck is supposed to look like. And for more experienced players, RW aggro isn't going to want cards that other red and white decks don't want, so it won't feel different or be an identifiable lane. "RG ramp" and "UG ramp" can run into this problem a little as well if their ramp cards and expensive cards are too generically good and get sniped or splashed by other decks.

When Timmy does pick all the cards that say "threshold" and all the cards that say "mill" or "surveil", that needs to add up to a functional deck. That might be a fast deck with more cheap creatures and combat tricks, a slow deck with more card advantage and answers, or some other fundamentally sound idea like "fliers and defensive ground creatures" or "recurring a creature with a strong enter trigger".

Nonfunctional archetypes fall into a few categories. There's idea that are just non-starters in a limited context; some deck that relies on a density of effects that is just not feasible in draft. Usually this is inspired by some other format: lantern control, group hug, burn, something like that.

I also see a decent number of ideas that don't fit how limited decks are shaped. A limited deck usually wants about 17 lands, 15 creatures and 5 removal spells (this varies a bit format to format). This leaves about 3 slots for cards that are not any of those things. Any archetype that is asking you to reliably have access to cards that aren't creatures or removal (equipment, auras, vehicles, combat tricks) is going to run into problems. The usual solution is to have some mechanic that allows the thing you care about to occupy creature slots in your deck (job select, roles, adventures). Archetypes that ask you to do something that's fundamentally a bad idea in limited (discard cards without looting or rummaging, sacrificing lands) also fall into this category.

And I also see a lot of ideas that are theoretically supportable but aren't being given enough density to make it practical to put the synergy together. Most often these are things that "don't happen naturally" in a game of Magic and need specific enablers - cards leaving your graveyard, casting spells from outside your hand, activating nonmana abilities. My general rule of thumb is that for each common payoff you're giving a synergy package, you need four common enablers - which for things that don't happen naturally, usually means at least one mechanic that acts as an enabler, sometimes more. "BR sacrifice" and "WB sacrifice" are two archetypes that need more support than they're usually given. Sacrificing real nontoken creatures without death triggers to sacrifice outlets is generally not a good idea in limited.

<b>When to Make Your Draft Archetypes</b>

Because draft archetypes feature so heavily in WotC's marketing and design discussions, I've begun to see designers make them earlier and earlier in the design process, sometimes as the very first thing they do in a design. This is not a good idea. You should not decide on draft archetypes until at least you have a working set of mechanics. You can search for Task Triage on this site to see my recommended order for making a set.

Mechanics are the least flexible part of design. It's hard to come up with new mechanics, and there aren't a huge number of great mechanics left on the ground. It's way easier to build your draft archetypes around your mechanics than vice versa. If you start by making draft archetypes and try to come up with mechanics to fit you'll be setting yourself a difficult or even impossible task. It's easy to change your archetypes. If an archetype needs to go, it's maybe 5-10 cards with that text plus swapping out a few of the common enablers. Changing a mechanic is a bigger deal.

You should usually have ten draft archetypes sketched out before designing commons, though. Draft archetypes are a big influence on common design; one of the main roles of commons in modern sets is providing enough support for the draft archetypes.

<b>Sets Without Ten Draft Archetypes</b>

The ten-archetype structure is common, useful, and highly recommended, but it's not the only way to make a set. Here are some alternate structures.

A "guild set" has only five of the ten color pairs supported. Because more space in the set is devoted to each supported color pair, they can be made to play very differently, allowing for decks with devotion to synergy that wouldn't be possible in a ten-deck set. However, they tend to be less replayable than ten-deck sets and have less dynamic drafts. Wizards likes to say that they plant multiple different decks in each of the five supported pairs: 90% of the time, that's a lie.

A three-color set has a similar structure: five three-color groups are supported, with the five color pairs that are in two groups also being given an identity. Getting the balance right on these is very tricky (as in Wizards has pulled it off about one and a half times) but in the ideal case, there are five three color decks, five two color decks, and a five-color deck all viable.

A set can be built around monocolor themes that the two-color decks borrow from. The strongest example of this is DMU, where a white-blue deck could lean into the white token themes supported by blue cards or the blue instant / sorcery themes supported by white cards, or both.

In the opposite case, the set's synergy packages can be distributed among all the colors. In LTR, no matter which of the four viable colors you were in, you could lean into amass / tokens, ring-tempt, or noncreature spell synergies.

Draft archetypes are a structured and visible way of seeding synergy packages into your set, but the real synergy packages are usually not just the ten on the prerelease kit, and not just restricted to two colors. There's no need to adhere to any structure at all. You can have some small packages of just a few cards, some broad synergies that run through the whole set, even weird one-offs. This is the regular way that cubes are designed, and it can lead to deep (if hard to balance) environments.
